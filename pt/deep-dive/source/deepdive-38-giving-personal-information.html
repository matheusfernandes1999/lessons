🎯 Overview

Giving personal information is a fundamental skill in English communication. Whether you're filling out forms, introducing yourself in professional settings, making reservations, or dealing with official matters, you'll frequently need to provide details about yourself clearly and accurately.

This skill involves more than just knowing vocabulary—it requires understanding different formats for presenting information, pronunciation challenges with numbers and letters, cultural expectations about what information is appropriate to share in different contexts, and specific language patterns used when providing personal details.

For Brazilian learners, this area presents several challenges, including differences in document types, address formats, and spelling conventions. Additionally, the pronunciation of letters, numbers, and symbols can be particularly tricky, as can the appropriate level of formality when sharing personal information in different contexts.

🔍 Deep Dive

Providing personal information accurately is essential in many situations, from job applications to hotel check-ins. Let's explore the various aspects of giving personal information in English, with special attention to areas that often challenge Brazilian learners.

Names and Titles

Introducing Your Name:

When introducing yourself or providing your name, it's important to be clear and, if necessary, to spell it out:

  • "My name is [First Name] [Last Name]."

  • "I'm [First Name] [Last Name]."

  • "This is [First Name] [Last Name]."

For names that might be difficult for English speakers to pronounce or spell:

  • "My name is João Silva. That's J-O-A-O S-I-L-V-A."

  • "I'm Ana Lúcia Ferreira. Ana is spelled A-N-A, and Lúcia is L-U-C-I-A with an accent on the 'u', but you can just write it without the accent in English."

Name Order:

In English-speaking countries, the standard order is:

  • First name (given name)

  • Middle name(s) (if any)

  • Last name (family name/surname)

This differs from the Portuguese convention where multiple surnames may be used, and the mother's surname often precedes the father's surname.

Example:

  • Portuguese format: João Silva Oliveira (where Silva is the mother's surname and Oliveira is the father's surname)

  • English format: João Silva Oliveira (where Silva Oliveira is treated as the complete last name, or sometimes just Oliveira is used as the last name)

Titles and Forms of Address:

Common titles in English include:

  • Mr. (for men, regardless of marital status)

  • Mrs. (for married women)

  • Miss (for unmarried women, though less common now)

  • Ms. (for women regardless of marital status, now widely preferred)

  • Dr. (for those with doctoral degrees or medical doctors)

  • Prof. (for professors)

In forms, you might see:

  • Title: Mr./Mrs./Miss/Ms./Dr./Prof.

  • First Name/Given Name

  • Middle Name(s)

  • Last Name/Surname/Family Name

Common Challenges for Brazilian Learners:

  1. Multiple Surnames: Brazilians often have two or more surnames, which can cause confusion in English-speaking contexts where typically only one surname is expected.

    Solution: When filling out forms, you can either:

    • Include all surnames in the "Last Name" field

    • Use your last surname as your "Last Name" and include the others as additional "Middle Names"

  2. Compound First Names: Names like "João Pedro" or "Maria Eduarda" might be treated as first and middle names in English contexts.

    Solution: Clarify whether both parts form your first name or if the second part is a middle name.

  3. Accents and Special Characters: Portuguese names often contain accents (á, é, í, ó, ú, â, ê, ô, ç) which don't exist in English.

    Solution: When spelling your name, mention the accents but clarify that they can be omitted in English documents:

    • "My name is José, J-O-S-E with an accent on the 'e', but you can write it without the accent."

  4. Title Expectations: In Brazil, professional titles like "Doutor" are sometimes used as courtesy titles even for those without doctoral degrees, which isn't the case in English-speaking countries.

    Solution: Only use "Dr." if you have a doctoral degree or are a medical doctor.

The English Alphabet and Spelling

The English Alphabet:

A - Alpha/Ay B - Bravo/Bee C - Charlie/See D - Delta/Dee E - Echo/Ee F - Foxtrot/Eff G - Golf/Jee H - Hotel/Aitch I - India/Eye J - Juliet/Jay K - Kilo/Kay L - Lima/El M - Mike/Em N - November/En O - Oscar/Oh P - Papa/Pee Q - Quebec/Cue R - Romeo/Ar S - Sierra/Ess T - Tango/Tee U - Uniform/You V - Victor/Vee W - Whiskey/Double-you X - X-ray/Ex Y - Yankee/Why Z - Zulu/Zed (UK)/Zee (US)

Note: The first pronunciation for each letter is from the NATO phonetic alphabet, which is often used in official or technical contexts to avoid confusion. The second is the common name of the letter.

Spelling Out Names and Words:

When spelling out words, pronounce each letter clearly and pause slightly between letters:

  • "My email is silva.j@email.com. That's S-I-L-V-A dot J at E-M-A-I-L dot C-O-M."

  • "My last name is Albuquerque. That's A-L-B-U-Q-U-E-R-Q-U-E."

For clarity in difficult spellings, you can use words that begin with each letter:

  • "B as in Boy, R as in Romeo, A as in Alpha, Z as in Zebra, I as in India, L as in Lima."

Common Challenges for Brazilian Learners:

  1. Pronunciation of Specific Letters: Several English letters have pronunciations that differ significantly from their Portuguese counterparts:

    • E: Pronounced as "ee" in English, not "eh" as in Portuguese

    • I: Pronounced as "eye" in English, not "ee" as in Portuguese

    • G: Pronounced as "jee" in English, not "jeh" as in Portuguese

    • H: Pronounced as "aitch" in English, silent in Portuguese

    • J: Pronounced as "jay" in English, not "jota" as in Portuguese

    • R: Pronounced as "ar" in English, not "eh-heh" as in Portuguese

    • W: Pronounced as "double-you" in English, doesn't exist in Portuguese alphabet

    • Y: Pronounced as "why" in English, doesn't exist in Portuguese alphabet

  2. W and Y: These letters don't exist in the traditional Portuguese alphabet (though they're now officially included), so their pronunciation and use can be challenging.

  3. Letter Combinations: Certain letter combinations have specific pronunciations in English that differ from Portuguese:

    • "th" (as in "the" or "think")

    • "ch" (as in "chair")

    • "sh" (as in "ship")

    • "ph" (pronounced as "f" as in "phone")

  4. Double Letters: When spelling words with double letters, specify "double":

    • "My name is Anna with double N: A-N-N-A."

Numbers and Dates

Cardinal Numbers:

0 - zero 1 - one 2 - two 3 - three 4 - four 5 - five 6 - six 7 - seven 8 - eight 9 - nine 10 - ten 11 - eleven 12 - twelve 13 - thirteen 14 - fourteen 15 - fifteen 16 - sixteen 17 - seventeen 18 - eighteen 19 - nineteen 20 - twenty 21 - twenty-one 30 - thirty 40 - forty 50 - fifty 60 - sixty 70 - seventy 80 - eighty 90 - ninety 100 - one hundred 1,000 - one thousand 1,000,000 - one million 1,000,000,000 - one billion

Ordinal Numbers:

1st - first 2nd - second 3rd - third 4th - fourth 5th - fifth 6th - sixth 7th - seventh 8th - eighth 9th - ninth 10th - tenth 11th - eleventh 12th - twelfth 13th - thirteenth 20th - twentieth 21st - twenty-first 22nd - twenty-second 23rd - twenty-third 30th - thirtieth 100th - one hundredth

Phone Numbers:

In English, phone numbers are typically read digit by digit, with pauses at appropriate groupings:

  • US format: (555) 123-4567 Pronounced: "Five five five, one two three, four five six seven" Or: "Area code five five five, one two three, four five six seven"

  • UK format: 020 7946 0958 Pronounced: "Oh two oh, seven nine four six, oh nine five eight"

  • Brazilian format: (11) 98765-4321 In English, pronounced: "One one, nine eight seven six five, four three two one" Or: "Area code one one, nine eight seven six five, four three two one"

For international numbers, include the country code:

  • "+55 11 98765-4321" Pronounced: "Plus five five, one one, nine eight seven six five, four three two one" Or: "Country code five five, area code one one, nine eight seven six five, four three two one"

Dates:

Date formats differ significantly between countries:

  • US format: MM/DD/YYYY (Month/Day/Year) Example: 05/22/1985 = May 22, 1985

  • UK/International format: DD/MM/YYYY (Day/Month/Year) Example: 22/05/1985 = 22 May 1985

  • ISO standard: YYYY-MM-DD (Year-Month-Day) Example: 1985-05-22

When saying dates aloud:

  • US style: "May twenty-second, nineteen eighty-five" or "Five twenty-two, nineteen eighty-five"

  • UK style: "The twenty-second of May, nineteen eighty-five" or "Twenty-two May, nineteen eighty-five"

For years:

  • 1985: "nineteen eighty-five" (not "one thousand nine hundred and eighty-five")

  • 2023: "twenty twenty-three" or "two thousand (and) twenty-three"

Common Challenges for Brazilian Learners:

  1. Date Format Confusion: Brazil uses the DD/MM/YYYY format, which differs from the US format (MM/DD/YYYY). This can lead to misunderstandings, especially with dates like 05/04/2023 (April 5th in the US, May 4th in Brazil).

    Solution: When in doubt, write out the month name to avoid confusion: "April 5, 2023" or "5 April 2023."

  2. Pronunciation of Years: In Portuguese, years are typically read as whole numbers (e.g., 1985 as "mil novecentos e oitenta e cinco"), while in English, years are usually split into pairs (e.g., "nineteen eighty-five").

  3. Pronunciation of Specific Numbers: Some numbers in English have pronunciations that can be challenging:

    • "Fifteen" vs. "Fifty"

    • "Thirteen" vs. "Thirty"

    • "Fourteen" vs. "Forty"

    • "Sixteen" vs. "Sixty"

    • "Zero" vs. "Oh" (when reading phone numbers)

  4. Commas and Periods in Numbers: In Brazil, periods are used as thousand separators and commas for decimals (e.g., 1.000,50), while in English-speaking countries, it's the opposite (e.g., 1,000.50).

Addresses and Locations

Address Format:

The typical address format in English-speaking countries is:

Name Street Number + Street Name + Apartment/Unit Number (if applicable) City, State/Province Postal Code Country (if international)

Example: John Smith 123 Main Street, Apt 4B New York, NY 10001 USA

Differences from Brazilian Format:

  • In Brazil: Rua das Flores, 123, apto 45, Jardim Paulista, São Paulo, SP, CEP 01234-567

  • In English: 123 Rua das Flores, Apartment 45, Jardim Paulista, São Paulo, SP, 01234-567, Brazil

Key differences:

  1. Street number comes before street name in English

  2. "Apartment" or "Apt" instead of "apto"

  3. "ZIP code" or "Postal code" instead of "CEP"

  4. Country name added for international correspondence

Describing Your Address:

When saying your address aloud:

  • "I live at 123 Main Street, Apartment 4B, in New York City, New York, ZIP code 10001."

  • "My address is 123 Main Street, Apartment 4B, New York, NY 10001."

For Brazilian addresses when speaking in English:

  • "I live at 123 Rua das Flores, Apartment 45, in the Jardim Paulista neighborhood of São Paulo, SP, Brazil, postal code 01234-567."

  • "My address is 123 Rua das Flores, Apartment 45, Jardim Paulista, São Paulo, SP, 01234-567, Brazil."

Common Challenges for Brazilian Learners:

  1. Street Number Placement: In Brazil, the street name typically comes before the number (e.g., "Rua Augusta, 123"), while in English, the number comes first (e.g., "123 Augusta Street").

  2. Apartment Designation: In Brazil, "apto" or "apartamento" is used, while in English, "Apt" or "Apartment" is standard. Additionally, in some English-speaking countries, other terms like "Unit," "Suite," or "#" might be used.

  3. Floor Numbering: In the US, the ground floor is the first floor, while in Brazil (and the UK), the ground floor is followed by the first floor.

  4. Postal Code Terminology: "CEP" in Brazil vs. "ZIP code" (US) or "Postal code" (UK, Canada, Australia).

  5. State Abbreviations: Brazilian state abbreviations (e.g., SP, RJ, MG) are not widely recognized internationally, so it's often better to write out the state name in full when communicating with people from other countries.

Personal Documents and Identification

Common Identification Documents:

Different countries have different primary identification documents:

  • United States:

    • Social Security Number (SSN)

    • Driver's License

    • State ID Card

    • Passport

    • Birth Certificate

  • United Kingdom:

    • National Insurance Number (NIN)

    • Passport

    • Driving Licence

    • Birth Certificate

    • BRP (Biometric Residence Permit) for non-citizens

  • Canada:

    • Social Insurance Number (SIN)

    • Driver's License

    • Provincial ID Card

    • Passport

    • Birth Certificate

    • Health Card

  • Australia:

    • Tax File Number (TFN)

    • Driver's License

    • Medicare Card

    • Passport

    • Birth Certificate

Brazilian Documents in English:

When referring to Brazilian documents in English, it's helpful to provide a brief explanation:

  • CPF (Cadastro de Pessoas Físicas): "My CPF, which is the Brazilian individual taxpayer registry number, is 123.456.789-00."

  • RG (Registro Geral): "My RG, which is the Brazilian national ID card number, is 12.345.678-9."

  • Carteira de Habilitação: "My Brazilian driver's license number is 12345678900."

  • Comprovante de Residência: "I have a proof of residence, which is a utility bill showing my address."

  • Carteira de Trabalho: "My Brazilian work permit/employment record book number is 12345/67890."

  • Título de Eleitor: "My Brazilian voter registration number is 1234 5678 9012."

Common Challenges for Brazilian Learners:

  1. Document Equivalents: Brazilian documents like CPF and RG don't have exact equivalents in many English-speaking countries, making it challenging to explain their purpose.

    Solution: Provide a brief explanation of what the document is used for when mentioning it.

  2. Number Formatting: Brazilian documents often use specific formatting with periods and hyphens (e.g., CPF: 123.456.789-00), which may differ from local conventions.

    Solution: Be prepared to provide your number with or without the special formatting, depending on the system's requirements.

  3. Proof of Address Requirements: What constitutes acceptable proof of address varies by country. In some places, only certain types of utility bills or government correspondence are accepted.

    Solution: Ask specifically what types of documents are accepted as proof of address in that particular context.

  4. Birth Certificate Information: Brazilian birth certificates contain different information and formatting compared to those from English-speaking countries.

    Solution: When providing birth certificate information, be prepared to explain or clarify certain details that might not have direct equivalents.

Email Addresses and Social Media

Email Addresses:

When providing your email address verbally:

  • Specify "at" for @ symbol

  • Say "dot" for periods

  • Spell out any potentially confusing parts

  • Clarify uppercase/lowercase if necessary (though most systems are case-insensitive for email addresses)

Example:

  • For "joao.silva@email.com": "My email is joao dot silva at email dot com. That's J-O-A-O dot S-I-L-V-A at E-M-A-I-L dot C-O-M."

  • For "maria_2023@provider.net": "My email is maria underscore twenty twenty-three at provider dot net. That's M-A-R-I-A underscore 2-0-2-3 at P-R-O-V-I-D-E-R dot N-E-T."

Social Media Handles:

When providing social media handles:

  • Specify the platform

  • Mention any special characters

  • Spell out if necessary

Examples:

  • "My Instagram handle is @joao_silva_photos, that's J-O-A-O underscore S-I-L-V-A underscore P-H-O-T-O-S."

  • "You can find me on Twitter as @MariaTeach2023, that's M-A-R-I-A T-E-A-C-H twenty twenty-three."

Common Challenges for Brazilian Learners:

  1. Pronunciation of Symbols:

    • @ is "at" (not "arroba")

    • . is "dot" (not "ponto")

    • _ is "underscore" (not "sublinhado")

    • is "hyphen" or "dash" (not "traço")

    • / is "slash" or "forward slash" (not "barra")

    • \ is "backslash" (not "barra invertida")

  2. Username Conventions: Social media handles and usernames often use conventions that may differ from Brazilian norms, such as:

    • Omitting accents and special characters

    • Using underscores instead of spaces

    • Combining first and last names without separation

  3. Pronunciation of Domain Extensions:

    • .com is pronounced "dot com" (not "ponto com")

    • .org is "dot org" (not "ponto org")

    • .edu is "dot e-d-u" (not "ponto edu")

    • .gov is "dot gov" (not "ponto gov")

Occupation and Professional Information

Describing Your Occupation:

When asked about your job or profession:

  • "I'm a/an [job title]."

  • "I work as a/an [job title]."

  • "I work in [industry/field]."

  • "I'm employed by [company name] as a/an [job title]."

Examples:

  • "I'm a software engineer."

  • "I work as a marketing manager."

  • "I work in healthcare."

  • "I'm employed by Microsoft as a product designer."

For students:

  • "I'm a student at [institution name]."

  • "I'm studying [subject] at [institution name]."

  • "I'm in my [first/second/third/fourth] year of [program] at [institution name]."

Examples:

  • "I'm a student at the University of São Paulo."

  • "I'm studying Computer Science at UFRJ."

  • "I'm in my third year of Law at PUC-Rio."

Educational Qualifications:

When describing your educational background:

  • "I have a [degree type] in [subject] from [institution]."

  • "I graduated from [institution] with a [degree type] in [subject]."

  • "I hold a [degree type] in [subject]."

Examples:

  • "I have a Bachelor's degree in Economics from USP."

  • "I graduated from UFMG with a Master's in Engineering."

  • "I hold a PhD in Linguistics from Unicamp."

Common degree abbreviations:

  • BA/BS/BSc - Bachelor of Arts/Science

  • MA/MS/MSc - Master of Arts/Science

  • PhD - Doctor of Philosophy

  • MBA - Master of Business Administration

Common Challenges for Brazilian Learners:

  1. Job Title Equivalents: Some Brazilian job titles don't have exact equivalents in English or may have different connotations.

    Examples:

    • "Engenheiro" might be "Engineer" but could require specification of the type (Civil Engineer, Mechanical Engineer, etc.)

    • "Advogado" is "Lawyer" or "Attorney" (US)

    • "Médico" is "Doctor" or "Physician"

  2. Academic Degree Differences: The structure of academic degrees differs between Brazil and English-speaking countries.

    Examples:

    • "Bacharelado" is generally equivalent to "Bachelor's degree"

    • "Licenciatura" might be translated as "Bachelor's in Education" or "Teaching Degree"

    • "Mestrado" is "Master's degree"

    • "Doutorado" is "Doctoral degree" or "PhD"

  3. Professional Licensing Terminology: Professional licensing and certification systems vary significantly between countries.

    Example:

    • "OAB" (Ordem dos Advogados do Brasil) might be explained as "the Brazilian Bar Association certification"

  4. Company Types and Structures: Business entity types have different names and abbreviations.

    Examples:

    • "Ltda." (Limitada) is similar to "Ltd." (Limited) or "LLC" (Limited Liability Company)

    • "S.A." (Sociedade Anônima) is similar to "Inc." (Incorporated) or "PLC" (Public Limited Company)

Cultural Considerations

When providing personal information in different cultural contexts, it's important to be aware of varying expectations and norms:

Privacy Expectations:

  • United States: Americans may seem open about certain personal details (like their job or where they live) but can be very protective of financial information and their Social Security Number.

  • United Kingdom: Brits tend to be more reserved about personal information in general, especially financial details and their address.

  • Germany: Germans typically have strong privacy concerns and may be uncomfortable sharing personal information unless absolutely necessary.

  • Brazil vs. English-speaking Countries: Brazilians may be accustomed to providing their CPF number for many transactions (like shopping), which would seem unusual in many English-speaking countries where ID is requested less frequently.

Formality Levels:

  • In more formal contexts (job interviews, government offices, healthcare settings), use full names and more detailed information.

  • In casual settings (social gatherings, informal introductions), first names and general information are usually sufficient.

Information to Be Cautious About Sharing:

  • Full date of birth (month and day may be fine, but year can be sensitive)

  • Home address (especially in public settings or online)

  • Financial information

  • ID numbers (Social Security, passport number, etc.)

  • Personal health information

Appropriate Questions:

What's considered appropriate to ask varies by culture and context:

  • Questions about marital status, age, or income may be common in some cultures but considered inappropriate or even illegal in certain contexts in English-speaking countries (especially job interviews).

  • Questions about family or children are common small talk in Brazil but might be considered too personal in initial business interactions in some English-speaking countries.

Navigating Identity in a Global World

When Maria first arrived in Toronto for her graduate studies, she was prepared for academic challenges but hadn't anticipated the complexities of establishing her identity in a new country. As a Brazilian accustomed to the document-heavy bureaucracy of her home country, she found herself confused by the different systems and expectations in Canada.

Her first challenge came at the university registration office. When asked for her name, Maria stated, "Maria Luiza Santos Oliveira," following the Brazilian convention of including both her mother's and father's surnames. The administrator looked confused and asked which was her "last name." After some discussion, Maria decided to use "Oliveira" as her surname in Canada, though this felt like leaving part of her identity behind.

The confusion continued when filling out forms. Where Brazilian documents typically request "CPF" and "RG" numbers, Canadian forms asked for a Social Insurance Number (SIN) and provincial health card. Maria had to learn not only new document names but also new protocols for protecting this information. In Brazil, providing her CPF for purchases was routine, but when she offered her SIN to a cell phone company in Canada, the representative looked alarmed and explained that this number should only be shared with employers and government agencies.

Even something as simple as giving her address proved challenging. In Brazil, she would say "Rua Ipanema, 45, apartamento 302," but she quickly learned that in English, the number comes before the street name: "45 Ipanema Street, Apartment 302." The first time she ordered delivery to her new apartment, the food never arrived because she had given her address in the Brazilian format.

Phone numbers presented another adjustment. When asked for her Brazilian contact number, Maria recited it as she would in Brazil: "nove-oito-sete-seis..." The person recording it looked increasingly confused until Maria realized she needed to say "nine-eight-seven-six..." and group the digits differently.

Perhaps most surprising was the difference in email communication. In Brazil, Maria was accustomed to formal email greetings like "Prezado Senhor" and signing off with "Atenciosamente." Her first emails to professors in Canada maintained this formality, but she soon noticed that most responses began with simply "Hi Maria" and ended with just "Regards" or even "Thanks." She gradually adjusted her style to match the more casual tone while still maintaining professionalism.

The cultural differences extended to job applications as well. 🔁 Deep Review

Providing personal information accurately in English involves more than just vocabulary—it requires understanding cultural expectations, format differences, and pronunciation challenges. Here's a summary of key points:

  • Names: Be prepared to adapt Brazilian naming conventions to English formats, especially regarding multiple surnames

  • Spelling: Master the English alphabet pronunciation, particularly challenging letters like E, I, G, H, J, R, W, and Y

  • Numbers and dates: Be aware of different formats (MM/DD/YYYY in US vs. DD/MM/YYYY in UK and Brazil) and grouping conventions for phone numbers

  • Addresses: In English, street numbers come before street names, unlike in Portuguese

  • Documents: Brazilian documents like CPF and RG don't have exact equivalents in English-speaking countries, so be ready to explain their purpose

  • Digital information: Use proper terminology for symbols in email addresses (@ is "at," . is "dot")

  • Privacy considerations: Understand that privacy expectations differ between cultures, particularly regarding ID numbers and personal details

Brazilian learners often face challenges with pronunciation of specific letters and numbers, document equivalents, format differences, and cultural expectations around sharing personal information. Being aware of these differences and practicing the correct formats will help you communicate your personal information clearly and appropriately in English-speaking contexts.
