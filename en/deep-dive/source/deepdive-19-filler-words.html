🎯 Overview

Filler words are short expressions we use in speech to pause, think, or soften what we’re saying. They don’t usually carry meaning, but they help communication feel more natural and fluent.

Examples:

  • Well, I’m not sure.

  • It’s, like, really weird.

  • You know, I was thinking about that.

We use filler words:

  • To gain time while thinking

  • To sound more natural or less abrupt

  • To engage the listener or check for understanding

⚠️ Brazilian learners tend to either avoid filler words (making speech sound robotic), or translate fillers from Portuguese (like “tipo”, “né”) in ways that don't fit in English.

🔍 Deep Dive

Filler words are not grammatical necessities but are key to fluency and sounding natural. They often serve discourse functions, not lexical meaning.

1. Common English Filler Words

  • Well: introduces a thought or softens disagreement

  • Like: used as a hesitation or approximation

  • You know: checks for understanding or agreement

  • I mean: clarifies or restates

  • Actually: adds correction or surprise

  • So: transitions between ideas

  • Uh/Um: verbal pauses

2. Brazilian Equivalents and Common Errors

Portuguese

English Equivalent

Notes

tipo

like

Use with care — not in every phrase!

né?

you know? / right?

“You know?” works for shared understanding

então

so / well

“So” is natural as a transition

ahn...

um / uh

These exist in both languages but should not be overused

When NOT to Use Fillers

  • Formal writing

  • Business presentations (limit use)

  • Overuse makes speech vague or uncertain

📖 Reading Practice

“Well, It Just Happened”

I was walking to the store, and, like, suddenly this dog came out of nowhere. I mean, it wasn’t destructiveous or anything, but it scared me for a second. And then, you know, it just started following me. Well, I didn’t know what to do at first, but eventually, it went away. Honestly, it was kind of funny afterwards.

🔁 Deep Review

  • Filler words improve naturalness and fluency in spoken English.

  • They shouldn’t be overused, especially in formal contexts.

  • Direct translations from Portuguese often sound unnatural — be cautious with “tipo”, “né”, “então”.
